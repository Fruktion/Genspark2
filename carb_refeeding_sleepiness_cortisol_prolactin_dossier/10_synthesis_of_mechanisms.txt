INTEGRATED MODEL
The most defensible integrated model is a three-layer model:

LAYER 1: UPSTREAM FASTING / RESTRICTION STATE
- Complete carbohydrate restriction + caloric deficit induce a fasting-adapted state characterized by elevated cortisol, low-carbohydrate fuel use, and likely reduced carbohydrate oxidation readiness [S001,S006].
- Over time, skeletal muscle becomes less favorable for postprandial carbohydrate disposal via increased early PDK4 and later reduced GLUT4, INSR, and AMPK [S001].
- Thyroid adaptation may reduce T3, lowering energetic resilience [S008,S009].
- Prolactin/dopaminergic dysregulation may reduce arousal reserve in some individuals [S003,S007].

LAYER 2: ACUTE CARBOHYDRATE EXPOSURE
- Carbohydrate reintroduction produces a larger physiological perturbation than it would in a mixed-diet state because tissues are relatively adapted away from carbohydrate use [S001,S002].
- Blood glucose rises more strongly or is handled less smoothly.
- Insulin/C-peptide and incretin responses may be accentuated or poorly matched to tissue handling [S001,S002,S004].
- Acute CNS changes include orexin suppression and higher Trp/LNAA ratio favoring serotonin-related sleep promotion [S010].
- Autonomic balance shifts toward parasympathetic / satiety physiology.
- In some meal contexts, inflammatory and endotoxin signaling add fatigue-like load [S005].

LAYER 3: SUBJECTIVE SYMPTOM EMERGENCE
- The person experiences excessive sleepiness because multiple wake-promoting supports are simultaneously reduced: stress-driven alertness falls relatively, orexin signaling is suppressed, serotonin-related sleep pressure rises, and dopaminergic tone may already be suboptimal.
- If glucose later drops rapidly, this can deepen the “crash,” whether or not measured glucose meets formal hypoglycemia criteria.

PRIMARY VS SECONDARY MECHANISMS
Primary:
1. Metabolic inflexibility / impaired carbohydrate handling after prolonged restriction [S001].
2. HPA-axis activation from fasting/caloric deficit [S006].
3. Acute CNS sleep-promoting response to carbohydrate via orexin and tryptophan-related mechanisms [S010].

Secondary / amplifiers:
4. Prolactin-dopamine arousal reduction [S003].
5. Low-T3 adaptation / reduced energetic resilience [S008,S009].
6. Postprandial inflammation/endotoxin load [S005].
7. Circadian timing effects [S006,S010].

Tertiary / speculative modifiers:
8. GLA/PGE1 endocrine interactions [S011].
9. Oleic-acid-derived incretin/OEA modulation (currently low-confidence in this opened set).

TEMPORAL SEQUENCE
Fasting / restriction days-weeks:
- rising cortisol, low-carb adaptation, tissue signaling changes, possible low-T3 and prolactin/dopamine changes.
Minutes after carbohydrate:
- glucose rises, insulin rises, orexin suppression begins, parasympathetic shift begins.
30-120 minutes:
- subjective heaviness/somnolence may emerge from CNS/autonomic integration; larger in those with lower arousal reserve.
2-5 hours:
- if present, reactive decline or inflammatory fatigue mechanisms may deepen the symptom burden.

FEEDBACK LOOPS
1. Caloric deficit -> cortisol -> sleep disruption -> more vulnerability to postprandial sleepiness.
2. Low-carb adaptation -> reduced glucose tolerance -> stronger meal perturbation -> more aversive sleepiness -> re-avoidance of carbs -> persistence of adaptation.
3. Prolactin/dopamine dysregulation -> lower arousal -> more perceived crash after meals.

BOTTOM-LINE SYNTHESIS
No single universal winner is justified. The best explanation is a convergent systems model in which carbohydrate reintroduction collides with a stress-adapted, low-carbohydrate, lower-flexibility metabolic state and then is translated into excessive sleepiness by CNS arousal pathways.
