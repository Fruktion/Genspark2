TITLE
Neurotransmitter and CNS mechanisms
PURPOSE
Map central nervous system pathways through which carbohydrate ingestion, fasting adaptation, cortisol, and prolactin-related dopaminergic changes may alter wakefulness and sleep pressure.
STATUS
SUBSTANTIVE_DRAFT
SCOPE
Orexin/hypocretin, serotonin, melatonin, dopamine, histamine, GABA/glutamate, adenosine, and CNS integration of meal responses.

KEY_QUESTIONS
Which CNS pathways most plausibly explain excessive sleepiness after carbohydrate intake in this metabolic state?
EVIDENCE_TIER
Moderate; strongest for orexin and tryptophan/serotonin plausibility, weaker for exact daytime symptom mapping.

TOP-LINE ASSESSMENT
The CNS likely converts the peripheral refeeding event into the subjective symptom. The two most defensible CNS mechanisms are: (1) glucose- and insulin-related suppression of orexin/hypocretin wakefulness systems, and (2) increased brain tryptophan availability favoring serotonin/melatonin-related sleep-promoting signaling [S010]. Reduced dopaminergic tone related to elevated prolactin is a plausible amplifier of this response [S003].

1. OREXIN / HYPOCRETIN
Orexin neurons help maintain wakefulness and integrate energy state with arousal. S010 highlights that these neurons are glucose-sensitive [S010]. Acute carbohydrate ingestion can therefore directly reduce orexin-mediated wake drive. In a low-carbohydrate-adapted person, this may feel especially dramatic because baseline wakefulness may have been maintained partly through fasting-related counter-regulatory and orexin-linked states.

2. SEROTONIN AND MELATONIN
Carbohydrate-induced shifts in Trp/LNAA ratio can favor brain tryptophan uptake and downstream serotonin/melatonin synthesis [S010]. This is one of the classic mechanisms by which high-glycemic meals can reduce sleep onset latency. It likely does not explain the entire phenotype, but it gives a direct brain-level path from carbohydrate to sleepiness.

3. DOPAMINE
S003 supports the idea that elevated prolactin and reduced dopaminergic tone can worsen appetite regulation, reward signaling, and metabolic profile [S003]. Extending that to arousal is reasonable because dopamine is a core arousal/salience transmitter. If dopaminergic tone is already low, the wake-promoting system may be easier to suppress after a meal.

4. HISTAMINE
The currently opened source set does not directly address histamine in this phenotype. Histaminergic wake systems are important biologically, but specific claims here would be too inferential. Histamine should remain a background possibility rather than a highlighted mechanism at this stage.

5. GABA/GLUTAMATE
Likewise, carbohydrate intake can indirectly change excitatory/inhibitory CNS balance, but the current opened source base is insufficient for strong mechanistic claims specific to this phenotype. This remains a speculative secondary pathway.

6. ADENOSINE / SLEEP PRESSURE
Adenosine is a general homeostatic sleep-pressure signal. The key contribution here is not that carbohydrate uniquely generates adenosine, but that fasting/stress states may conceal accumulated sleep pressure, while postprandial changes reveal it. This is a plausible synthesis-level amplifier rather than an established primary trigger in the source set.

BOTTOM-LINE JUDGMENT
Primary CNS mechanisms: orexin suppression and serotonin-related sleep promotion [S010].
Probable amplifier: reduced dopaminergic tone associated with prolactin dysregulation [S003].
Secondary/speculative: histamine, GABA/glutamate, adenosine interactions.


[ROUND 2 ADDITION]
7. HUMAN MEAL-TIMING DATA SUPPORT A STRONGER ROLE FOR CNS REWARD/AROUSAL CONTEXT THAN FOR HOMEOSTATIC HUNGER ALONE
S022 showed that the hedonic drive to eat was higher in the evening, while the homeostatic drive was not equivalently shifted, and that evening carbohydrate-rich meals produced larger glucose and insulin excursions [S022]. This supports a broader CNS framing: the brain state into which carbohydrate is delivered changes the experiential outcome. The same meal can be processed differently depending on circadian and reward context.

8. IL-1 CONTRIBUTION IMPLIES THAT CNS FATIGUE PATHWAYS ARE NOT JUST OREXIN/SEROTONIN
S015 showed that blocking IL-1 signaling reduced postprandial fatigue [S015]. Because systemic CRP and IL-6 did not change significantly, the result is consistent with the possibility that centrally relevant cytokine signaling or local inflammatory signaling can alter vigilance even when standard peripheral markers appear quiet.

9. ROUND-2 JUDGMENT UPDATE
The CNS model now has three better-grounded layers:
- orexin/hypocretin suppression by glucose [S010],
- serotonin-related sleep promotion via Trp/LNAA shifts [S010],
- cytokine-linked central fatigue signaling via IL-1 pathways [S015].
Dopamine/prolactin remains an amplifier rather than a principal final common pathway.
