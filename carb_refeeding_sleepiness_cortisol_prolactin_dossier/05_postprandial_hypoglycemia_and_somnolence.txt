TITLE
Postprandial hypoglycemia and somnolence
PURPOSE
Assess whether reactive hypoglycemia, rapid glycemic decline, incretin/insulin dynamics, and known postprandial sleepiness pathways explain the reported excessive sleepiness after carbohydrate reintroduction.
STATUS
SUBSTANTIVE_DRAFT
SCOPE
Reactive hypoglycemia, first-phase insulin issues, second-phase overshoot, orexin, serotonin/melatonin, vagal signaling, cytokines, and timing of symptoms.

KEY_QUESTIONS
Is true reactive hypoglycemia necessary? Which post-meal mechanisms are most likely to explain the symptom timing? How should blood-flow theories be rated?

TOP-LINE ASSESSMENT
“Reactive hypoglycemia” is an important candidate but should not be treated as the only explanation. The best current model is broader: carbohydrate reintroduction after low-carbohydrate adaptation may produce exaggerated postprandial glucose rise, altered insulin/incretin dynamics, orexin suppression, increased brain tryptophan availability, parasympathetic dominance, and possibly inflammatory signaling. Excessive sleepiness can emerge even without documented biochemical glucose values below the classic hypoglycemia threshold.

1. REACTIVE HYPOGLYCEMIA: WHAT IS ESTABLISHED
S004 summarizes the classical framework in which impaired early (first-phase) insulin release allows higher initial glucose excursion, followed by delayed and excessive second-phase insulin secretion that can drive late postprandial hypoglycemia [S004]. This model is most relevant when symptoms occur 2-5 hours after carbohydrate intake. It is not ideal for explaining sleepiness occurring within minutes of eating.

Thus the timing matters:
- 15-60 minutes: less likely to be classic late reactive hypoglycemia.
- 2-5 hours: much more compatible with delayed hyperinsulinemic decline.

2. FIRST-PHASE INSULIN / DELAYED SECOND-PHASE OVERSHOOT
The classic late-RH model remains useful because it explains why some individuals can feel worst not at peak glucose but during the later decline. S004 notes that diminished first-phase release can permit sharper early hyperglycemia, which then drives delayed excessive later insulin secretion [S004]. If the person is low-carb adapted and peripheral tissues are relatively less ready to clear glucose smoothly [S001], this sequence may be accentuated.

However, the dossier must be honest that direct proof of this exact sequence in the target phenotype is lacking. It is a probable mechanism, not yet an established one for this specific state.

3. EXAGGERATED POSTPRANDIAL RESPONSE AFTER LOW-CARB ADAPTATION
S001 found increased postprandial glucose response to a mixed meal after ketogenic adaptation, plus higher week-4 C-peptide after carbohydrate restriction [S001]. S002 found increased insulin/C-peptide response during OGTT under weight-maintaining ketogenic feeding in T2D [S002]. These findings are consistent with the idea that carbohydrate exposure after ketogenic adaptation can provoke a more pronounced endocrine response than expected from fasting glucose alone.

This does not prove reactive hypoglycemia. But it does strongly support a state in which a carbohydrate meal may feel qualitatively more disruptive.

4. OREXIN / HYPOCRETIN SUPPRESSION
S010 emphasizes that orexin neurons are glucose-sensitive and centrally important for wakefulness [S010]. High glucose tends to suppress orexin activity, whereas low glucose activates orexin neurons. Therefore an acute rise in glucose after carbohydrate intake can directly reduce wake-promoting tone. In a low-carb-adapted person who has spent substantial time in a low-glucose, fat-fueled state, this orexin-related wakefulness system may react strongly to carbohydrate exposure.

If the symptom occurs rapidly after carbohydrate ingestion, orexin suppression is a stronger candidate than classic delayed reactive hypoglycemia.

5. TRYPTOPHAN / SEROTONIN / MELATONIN PATHWAY
S010 reviews the longstanding mechanism by which carbohydrate-induced insulin action drives competing large neutral amino acids into muscle more than tryptophan, increasing the plasma Trp/LNAA ratio and facilitating brain tryptophan entry [S010]. That can favor serotonin and later melatonin synthesis. In sleep studies, high-glycemic meals can shorten sleep onset latency [S010].

Important nuance: those studies usually examine pre-sleep conditions and sleep architecture, not necessarily daytime post-meal hypersomnolence. So this mechanism is best seen as a probable CNS contributor rather than a complete explanation.

6. PARASYMPATHETIC / VAGAL ACTIVATION
Any mixed meal promotes satiety signaling and parasympathetic activity. In a person emerging from a high-cortisol fasting state, this shift may feel disproportionately sedating. Direct opened evidence is limited, but autonomic transition remains a biologically coherent amplifier, especially for early post-meal drowsiness.

7. IL-1 / CYTOKINE-MEDIATED FATIGUE
S005 shows that high-fat/high-carbohydrate meals can raise inflammatory signals including IL-6, TNF-alpha, LPS, NF-kB, ROS, and leukocyte activation in humans [S005]. IL-1 and cytokine signaling are classically linked to “sickness behavior,” including reduced motivation and increased sleep drive. This pathway is a better explanation for fatigue developing over a somewhat longer postprandial window than for immediate sleepiness after a small carb exposure.

8. BLOOD-FLOW REDISTRIBUTION THEORY
The common lay claim that people get sleepy because blood is “diverted to the gut” is weak and insufficient. It does not explain why carbohydrate-specific reintroduction in a low-carb-adapted state should be especially soporific, nor does it account for hormonal, neurotransmitter, glycemic, and inflammatory evidence. At most, splanchnic hemodynamic changes are minor adjuncts, not primary causes.

9. WHY SLEEPINESS MAY APPEAR IN THIS ADAPTED STATE SPECIFICALLY
This is the core synthesis:
- During prolonged carbohydrate restriction, tissues adapt away from carbohydrate use [S001,S002].
- Carbohydrate reintroduction then produces a larger physiological perturbation than it would in a mixed-diet person.
- The carbohydrate meal simultaneously raises glucose, alters insulin/incretin signaling, suppresses orexin, increases tryptophan availability to the brain, and shifts autonomic tone.
- If glucose later falls rapidly, even without frank hypoglycemia, the person may experience a “crash” state with sleepiness, lethargy, or reduced vigilance.

Thus the low-carb-adapted state increases the odds that ordinary postprandial sleepiness mechanisms become excessive.

10. RED TEAM / HOW THIS COULD BE WRONG
- Some suspected reactive hypoglycemia patients have no measurable metabolic disturbance on modern testing. True; S004 itself acknowledges diagnostic complexity.
- Sleepiness may occur without any measurable glucose nadir. Also true; orexin/serotonin/autonomic mechanisms can explain this.
- Meal size and total calories may matter as much as carbohydrates. Yes, especially for mixed meals.
- The symptom could be mainly circadian, with carbohydrate acting as a timing-sensitive trigger rather than a primary metabolic cause.

BOTTOM-LINE JUDGMENT
Established:
- Postprandial sleepiness has multiple plausible pathways, not just reactive hypoglycemia [S010].
- Low-carbohydrate adaptation can exaggerate postprandial glucose response [S001,S002].
Probable:
- Excessive sleepiness in this phenotype is often driven by the combination of impaired carbohydrate tolerance, orexin suppression, and CNS/autonomic postprandial effects.
Possible:
- In some individuals, delayed excessive insulin response and genuine reactive hypoglycemia contribute substantially, especially when symptoms occur 2-5 hours after the meal [S004].


[ROUND 2 ADDITION]
11. DIRECT HUMAN EVIDENCE THAT POSTPRANDIAL FATIGUE IS NOT ONLY A GLUCOSE STORY
S015 adds decisive human experimental evidence that IL-1 signaling contributes to postprandial fatigue. In lean and obese men given a heavy high-fat/high-carbohydrate meal after a run, fatigue increased within 30 minutes of the meal, and IL-1 receptor antagonism with anakinra reduced postprandial fatigue, especially in obese participants [S015]. This is important because classic late reactive hypoglycemia cannot fully explain fatigue that begins so early. It strengthens a model in which inflammatory or CNS cytokine signaling contributes alongside glucose dynamics.

12. THE STRONGEST ROUND-2 NULL STUDY: GLUCOSE RHYTHMS CAN SHIFT WITHOUT SUBJECTIVE SLEEPINESS CHANGING
S021 found that delaying meals shifted plasma glucose rhythms substantially but did not change subjective sleepiness [S021]. This is crucial red-team evidence. It means glucose changes alone are insufficient; the phenotype likely requires an interaction among glycemic, circadian, inflammatory, meal-composition, and CNS factors.

13. SCOPING-REVIEW LESSON: VERY FEW STUDIES CO-MEASURE GLUCOSE AND SLEEPINESS WELL
S014 found that very few modern studies simultaneously measured blood glucose, sleepiness, and productivity or performance directly, and several interventions reduced fatigue without changing glucose materially [S014]. This supports the dossier’s anti-gaming rule: absence of a direct glucose-sleepiness correlation in one episode would not falsify meal-triggered sleepiness.

14. HISTORICAL HUMAN ADJACENT STUDIES STILL POINT TOWARD MEAL-COMPOSITION EFFECTS
Search-pass additions S023-S025 indicate older human studies in which meal composition altered postprandial sleepiness/fatigue or performance, including signals that pure carbohydrate meals can increase subjective fatigue and slow reaction time [S023-S025]. These are abstract-only and therefore non-decisive, but they provide convergent historical human context that post-meal sleepiness is composition-sensitive.

15. ROUND-2 JUDGMENT UPDATE
The strongest updated model is now:
- early post-meal sleepiness/fatigue: orexin suppression, serotonin/Trp shifts, autonomic change, and inflammatory signaling are stronger candidates than classic reactive hypoglycemia alone.
- later crash symptoms: delayed glycemic decline and reactive hypoglycemia remain plausible, especially when symptoms are delayed by 2-5 hours.
- glucose metrics matter, but they do not alone determine subjective sleepiness.
