STATE UNDER INVESTIGATION
The target state is a combined metabolic-neuroendocrine phenotype with these defining features: (1) sustained caloric deficit; (2) complete or near-complete carbohydrate restriction sufficient to induce ketosis/adaptation to fat-based fuel use; (3) protein intake around 2 g/kg/day; (4) fat intake around 1 g/kg/day with an unusually specific composition enriched in EPA+DHA, gamma-linolenic acid (GLA), and oleic acid; (5) fasting-state elevation of cortisol and prolactin; and (6) marked excessive sleepiness after carbohydrates are reintroduced. The question is not merely why carbohydrate intake can make some people drowsy in general, but why this happens excessively in a person already adapted to fasting/low-carbohydrate energy metabolism and already showing hormonal evidence of stress-axis activation and altered lactotroph/dopaminergic regulation.

PRIMARY PHENOTYPE TO EXPLAIN
The core symptom is post-carbohydrate somnolence. For this dossier, that means a reproducible, disproportionately strong increase in sleep pressure, lethargy, reduced vigilance, or urge to sleep after eating carbohydrate-containing food, beyond ordinary satiety. The phrase “after consumption of carbohydrates starts becoming excessively sleepy” implies a state-dependent change: minimal or less-prominent sleepiness during fasting/strict carbohydrate restriction, but prominent sleepiness after carbohydrate exposure. This points toward a refeeding-specific interaction between prior adaptation state and acute meal response.

KEY VARIABLES
1. Complete carbohydrate restriction:
- Relevant because human low-carbohydrate/ketogenic adaptation can change skeletal muscle glucose handling, respiratory quotient, counter-regulatory hormone balance, thyroid hormone patterns, and brain/gut signaling [S001, S002, S009, S010].
2. Caloric deficit:
- Relevant because calorie deficit independently activates the HPA axis, lowers leptin, can lower T3, and alters prolactin/thyrotroph responses [S006, S008, S009].
3. High protein intake (~2 g/kg):
- Relevant because high protein changes gluconeogenic flux, incretin response, insulin response, tryptophan/LNAA competition, and amino-acid inflammatory signaling [S005, S010].
4. Fat intake (~1 g/kg) from EPA+DHA, GLA, oleic acid:
- Relevant because these fatty acids can modify inflammation, membrane composition, incretin pathways, eicosanoid balance, and possibly stress-axis tone, but the combined-profile evidence is mostly indirect [S005, S011].
5. Elevated fasting cortisol:
- Relevant because cortisol increases during fasting and low-energy/carbohydrate states, can alter glucose production, sleep-wake biology, and meal responses [S006].
6. Elevated fasting prolactin:
- Relevant because prolactin interacts bidirectionally with dopamine, appetite/arousal systems, and metabolic control; however direct fasting-human evidence is weaker and more heterogeneous than for cortisol [S003, S007].

MECHANISTIC DOMAINS REQUIRED
1. Metabolic adaptation:
- Ketosis, tissue insulin sensitivity distribution, muscle glucose transporter content, pyruvate dehydrogenase regulation, glycogen, gluconeogenesis, hepatic versus peripheral glucose handling.
2. Hormonal/endocrine:
- Cortisol/HPA axis, prolactin-dopamine axis, thyroid adaptation, glucagon, catecholamines, incretins.
3. Neurotransmitter/CNS:
- Orexin/hypocretin, serotonin, melatonin, dopamine, histamine, GABA/glutamate, adenosine.
4. Inflammatory/immune:
- Meal-induced IL-1/IL-6/TNF-alpha, NF-kB, ROS, endotoxin/LPS, sickness-behavior overlap [S005].
5. Circadian/chronobiological:
- Meal timing, cortisol rhythm, wakefulness drive, sleep debt, chronotype, postprandial timing effects [S006, S010].
6. Autonomic/hemodynamic/gut-brain:
- Parasympathetic activation, gastric emptying, vagal signaling, incretin signaling, possible reactive hypoglycemia or rapid glycemic decline.

WHAT COUNTS AS A CAUSE VS CONTRIBUTOR VS CORRELATION
Cause:
A cause in this dossier is a mechanism that is both biologically capable of producing the phenotype and has evidence linking it to the relevant state transition. Example: low-carbohydrate adaptation reducing glucose tolerance and lowering muscle GLUT4/insulin-receptor content after weeks of ketogenic feeding is a candidate causal mechanism because it can plausibly change the physiological response to carbohydrate reintroduction [S001].

Contributor:
A contributor is a mechanism that plausibly amplifies or biases the state without being sufficient alone. Example: elevated prolactin lowering dopaminergic tone may reduce arousal and worsen subjective sleepiness, but by itself may not fully explain why sleepiness is triggered specifically by carbohydrate intake [S003].

Correlation:
A correlation is an associated observation without sufficient mechanistic or temporal evidence. Example: an elevated lab value measured during fasting could be incidental, compensatory, or downstream. For major claims, correlation alone is insufficient.

CAUSAL STANDARD USED HERE
The dossier uses a layered standard:
- Established mechanism: replicated human evidence or strong human-plus-mechanistic coherence.
- Probable contributor: indirect but convergent evidence from human studies plus mechanistic plausibility.
- Speculative possibility: biologically coherent but not directly evidenced in this exact phenotype.

WORKING HYPOTHESIS
The leading working model is that prolonged carbohydrate restriction plus caloric deficit creates a fasting-adapted, cortisol-elevated state in which peripheral glucose handling becomes transiently less efficient on carbohydrate re-exposure, while central arousal systems are simultaneously made more vulnerable by stress-axis activation, possible low-T3 adaptation, and prolactin/dopamine changes. Carbohydrate ingestion then triggers a mixed response: insulin and incretin signaling, altered glucose disposal, orexin suppression, increased tryptophan availability to the brain, autonomic shift toward parasympathetic tone, and sometimes inflammatory/endotoxin signaling. Excessive sleepiness emerges from the convergence of these pathways rather than from a single “food coma” explanation.

EXCLUSIONS / NON-GOALS
This dossier is not primarily about:
- Idiopathic chronic fatigue unrelated to meals.
- Narcolepsy or primary central hypersomnia unless discussed as differential-like boundary conditions.
- Diabetes treatment generally, except where directly relevant to reactive/postprandial physiology.
- Product or supplement recommendation beyond evidence review.
